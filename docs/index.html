The short version

The Flat Earth app contains no flat-Earth model. Every view in it is calculated with standard globe astronomy, and the flat map is one of several ways the app can draw the same globe data.

We installed the free Android version and read how it works. Three things stand out:

  • The sky view is a planetarium. It places each star using your latitude and the time, with the same formula any astronomy program uses.
  • The flat map is a globe, unrolled. Its daylight shading, its star positions and its eclipse shadows are all worked out in latitude and longitude on a sphere, then drawn flat.
  • The two views cannot be derived from each other. The sky view gives the right answer. The flat map, taken as a real place, gives a different one.

What the app shows you

The app has two views, and you switch between them with one button.

ViewWhat you seeWhat it suggests
Map viewA flat, circular map with the North Pole at the centre. The Sun, Moon and stars sit over it and circle the pole.The Sun and stars move in circles above a flat Earth.
Sky viewThe sky from a place you choose, at a time you choose, with a horizon and a landscape.You have flown in and are now looking up from the map.

The question we asked: is the sky view what you would see from inside the map view? It is not. They are two separate programs that share a clock and your location.

What is inside

Four parts of the app do the work, and all four use a round Earth.

Each excerpt below is marked by where it runs: sky view, map view, or shared by both.

1. The stars in the sky view. For each star the app takes its catalogue position, your latitude and the time, and works out how high it is and in which direction. This is the textbook conversion used by every planetarium. Nothing from the flat map goes into it: no map position, no height above a plane, no distance across the map.

float ha = lst - mra;
...
float sinAlt = sinLat * sinDec + cosLat * cosDec * cosHA;
...
float az = atan(-cosDec * sinHA, (sinDec - sinLat * sinAlt) / cosLat);
AzimAlt=raDecl_to_AltAz(mra,mdec,LatLonPresTemp.x,LST).yx;

Sky view. From Shader/StarMesh3D.gdshader. mra and mdec are the star's catalogue position, LatLonPresTemp.x is your latitude and LST is local star time. Those four values are everything the star's position depends on.

2. Day and night on the flat map. For every point on the map, the app works out how high the Sun is at that latitude and longitude on a sphere. Above the horizon is drawn as day, below as night. The lit shape on the map is a globe's day side, drawn flat.

//x: Greenwich Hour Angle, y: sun decl
uniform vec2 sun_data;

float GetAlt(vec2 LonLat) {
    float HA = sun_data.x + LonLat.x;
    ...
    float sinAlt = sinLat * sinDec + cosLat * cosDec * cosHA;
    return asind(sinAlt);
}

Map view. From Shader/include/sun.gdshaderinc. This runs for every point on the map. The script that feeds it passes the Sun as two angles and nothing else:

var GHA = (TimeEngine.GST - Astronomy.Sun.ApparentGeocentricRA) * 15.0
fe_mesh_mat.set_shader_parameter("sun_data", Vector2(GHA, Astronomy.Sun.ApparentGeocentricDeclination))

Map view. From Script/MapView.gd (recovered script). The map's Sun has a direction but no height and no distance. The icon on the map marks where that direction is straight overhead.

3. The map itself. The Earth picture is an ordinary latitude and longitude image. The app pushes it through a projection, the same kind of maths an atlas uses to flatten a globe onto a page. The flat circular map is one setting. The app's own list of projections also includes “orthographic”, which is the Earth drawn as a ball.

vec2 UV_to_spherical(vec2 uv) {
    ...
    float lon = x * PI;        // Longitude from -PI to PI
    float lat = y * PI*0.5;  // Latitude from -PI/2 to PI/2
    return vec2(lon, lat);
}

Map view. From Shader/include/commons.gdshaderinc: every point of the map picture is first turned back into longitude and latitude. The projection names stored in Script/data/ProjData.gd are: Azimuthal Equidistant, Equirectangular, Extrapolated Equidistant, Extrapolated Equidistant2, Azimuthal EqualArea, orthographic, Cassini.

The two tropics come out the same size. On the map itself the tropics are drawn by latitude alone, at 23.5 degrees north and south. Nothing in the app gives the map a distance scale.

float cancer    = 1.0-latitudeLine(geo_pos,  23.5,  0.8, true,  lw);   // 0.8° dashed
float capricorn = 1.0-latitudeLine(geo_pos, -23.5,  0.8, true, lw);

Map view. From Shader/Projection.gdshader, lines 406 and 407.

The eclipse code is the one place where the app has to commit to real sizes on the Earth. To work out where the Moon's shadow falls it needs each place's distance from the Earth's axis, and it takes that from a function of latitude built on the cosine. A cosine is the same for a latitude north or south, so the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn get the same value.

highp float RhoCosThetaPrime(highp float latDeg, highp float heightM) {
    highp float lat = DEG2RAD * latDeg;
    highp float U = atan(FLATTENING * tan(lat));
    return cos(U) + (heightM / EARTH_RADIUS_M * cos(lat));
}

Map view. From Shader/Projection.gdshader, lines 94 to 98, used with EARTH_RADIUS_KM in the lines that follow.

The middle column is 2π × 6378.14 km × the function above at 23.5 degrees, which is 5,852 km from the axis. The app does not store that figure; it is what its formula evaluates to. The right column assumes the usual scale for this kind of map, about 111 km per degree measured out from the pole. The app states no scale, but its drawing does fix the ratio: the Capricorn ring is 1.71 times as long as the Cancer ring.

The star catalogue it ships (83,500 stars) is the standard HYG catalogue, which lists each star's distance. The scripts ask the planet engine for Sun-centred positions and convert between kilometres and astronomical units.

Why the stars turn the other way in the south

The app never sets a direction of rotation. There is no “clockwise” or “counter-clockwise” in it, and no rule for the southern hemisphere. The direction falls out of the geometry, and the two views use different geometry.

On the map, each star is drawn over the spot on Earth where it is directly overhead. As the clock runs, every star's spot slides west by the same amount. On the flat circular map that looks like one disc of stars turning around the North Pole. One centre, one direction, the same for everybody.

ra_n=repeat(ra_n+GST-12.0,24.0);
...
VERTEX = VERTEX * (quad_scale * local_quad_scale) + fe_mesh_project(uv,transition);

Map view. From Shader/StarMesh2D.gdshader. GST is Greenwich star time. It is added to every star's position alike, then the star is placed with fe_mesh_project, the same function that places the land. Latitude does not appear.

In the sky view, the app adds one thing the map never uses: your latitude. Latitude sets how the sky's turning axis is tilted against your horizon.

  • In London the north end of the axis points 51.5 degrees up in the north. The stars circle it counter-clockwise.
  • In Sydney the same formula, with a negative latitude, puts the south end of the axis 34 degrees up in the south. The same turning, seen from the other end, runs clockwise.
  • At the equator both ends sit on the horizon and stars rise straight up in the east.
GST = Astronify.GreenwitchSiderealtime(JD_TT, true)
...
RenderingServer.global_shader_parameter_set("LST", Astronomy.astro.LocalSiderealTime(TimeEngine.GST, User.Location.LONGITUDE))

Shared. From Script/TimeEngine.gd (recovered script): the clock both views share. The sky view then combines it with latitude in the formula shown earlier, and the Milky Way background does the same:

vec3 eq_dir = eyedir_to_equatorial(eyedir, LatLonPresTemp.x, -LST);

Sky view. From Shader/sky.gdshader. A search of every script and shader for “hemisphere”, “clockwise” or a test on the sign of latitude finds nothing that sets a direction.

So the southern rotation is not programmed in. It is what a round-Earth formula gives when the latitude is negative.

This is the part a flat Earth cannot supply. Latitude can only tilt the sky's axis if “up” points in a different direction at each latitude. On a flat surface everyone's “up” is parallel. A flat Earth could put the centre of rotation farther away, but could not tilt it, and could never bring a second centre into view in the south.

The fly-in is a zoom and a cut

Pressing the sky button looks like flying down into the map. In the code it is three steps:

  1. The flat map zooms toward your location.
  2. While that plays, a separate 3D sky scene loads in the background.
  3. When the zoom ends, the map is hidden and the sky scene is shown.
var gpos = MapProjection.instance.Project(uv)
...
var tn: = MainCam2D.instance.move_to_object(gpos, 30.0, cd)
Utility.load_scene_SkyView()
if tn != null and tn.is_valid() and tn.is_running():
    await tn.finished
    MapView.instance.visible = false
    Ui.sky_view_ui_enable(true)

Shared. From Script/bottom_bar.gd (recovered script), the handler for the sky button: zoom the 2D camera, load the sky scene, wait for the zoom, hide the map.

The two scenes have different cameras. Only the clock and your latitude and longitude pass from one to the other. Nothing on the map, including the Sun and star positions drawn over it, is carried into the sky.

The Moon: one phase, three ways of turning it

The Moon's phase is computed correctly and is the same in every view. What differs is which way up it is drawn, and on the flat map the lit side does not face the map's own Sun.

One shader draws the Moon everywhere, as a lit ball: it is told which point of the Moon faces the Earth and which point has the Sun overhead. That is real three-body geometry, and it is why the phase is right. Then one line decides how far to turn the picture on screen:

data2gpu[vData.PA] = wrapf(sso.position_angle - (sso.ParallacticAngle if EnableParallacticAngle else 0.0), 0.0, 360.0)

Shared. From Script/Utility.gd (recovered script). ParallacticAngle is how far the sky is turned for one particular observer. It is worked out from your latitude, your longitude and the time.

ViewTurned for the observer?Result
Sky viewAlwaysCorrect for where you stand
Moon tracking viewA switch the user can setOn: as you see it. Off: celestial north at the top
Flat mapNever. The code passes a fixed falseOne picture for everyone, with its top pointed at the map's North Pole

So the Moon on the flat map is the view from the centre of the Earth. It is nobody's actual view, and the map has no way to do better: the same Moon looks turned by a different amount from every place on Earth, and a single flat picture can only show one.

What we measured. Three screen captures of the map, with the app's location set to London, then Sydney, then Cape Town. The Moon icon's lit side sat at the same bearing from map-north each time: 256, 254 and 254 degrees, against a calculated 253 to 254. Changing where you are changes nothing on the map.

Why it misses. In the sky, the Moon's lit side points at the Sun. The flat circular map does not keep those directions, so once the picture is placed on the map, its lit side points somewhere else. Over one lunar month the miss runs from almost nothing at new Moon to 50 to 85 degrees around the quarters and the gibbous phases.

If the Moon on the map were a ball lit by the Sun on the map, its bright side would face that Sun. It was computed for a Sun about 150 million km away, and then placed on the map.

A fair reply is that the map Moon is only an icon for tonight's phase. That is true, and it is the point: the phase it shows needs a round Moon and a distant Sun to calculate, and turning it the right way up needs the observer's latitude.

What this does and does not show

It shows that the app has no flat model inside it. Every view is globe astronomy. The flat look comes from the choice of map projection.

It does not show that the developer is hiding this. The evidence points the other way:

  • The code is not disguised. The drawing code ships as readable text with the developer's own comments.
  • The Google Play listing says the map and the sky run on the same astronomical model and the same clock.
  • The app can show right ascension, declination, distance and angular size for each object.
  • The developer's site calls it a “geocentric presentation”. We found no statement from the developer that the Earth is flat.

The criticism that holds is narrower. The app is named Flat Earth and sold to a flat-Earth audience, and reviews on the App Store show users taking it as confirmation. Nothing in the listings tells them that the sky view cannot come from the map.

It does not, by itself, prove the shape of the Earth. A flat-Earth reply is that the formulas are only patterns fitted to what we observe. The answer to that is the table above: no single flat layout reproduces what the sky view shows, which is why the app has to use the globe formulas to get the sky right.
